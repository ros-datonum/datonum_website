AI lead and opportunity scoring

Turns inconsistent inbound inquiries into a consistent qualification decision — with the reasoning recorded, and a route to a person whenever the case is unclear.

The situation it addresses

Inquiries arrive with different levels of detail. What matters is buried inside descriptions, budgets, timelines, and previous correspondence. Reviewing each one by hand takes time and produces different judgments on different days. A simple points-based score misses the context that decides it.

Where AI is required

Reading a discursive inquiry and assessing fit, commercial potential, and where the difficulties lie — then explaining that assessment in terms a person can check. No rule can read an email and work out what the sender actually needs.

How it runs
  1. 01Inquiry captured — form, email, marketplace, or CRM
  2. 02Mapped into one consistent record
  3. 03Fixed rules applied — your must-haves and exclusions
  4. 04AI assesses fit, potential, and difficulty
  5. 05Priority calculated from weighted factors
  6. 06Uncertain or contradictory? Routed to a person, with the reason
  7. 07CRM updated with the score, the reasons, and the next action

Decision output: Strong fit · Review required · Low priority · Reject

What makes it trustworthy

The rules you set are applied separately from the AI assessment, so a must-have cannot be argued away. Every score carries its evidence, the risks identified, and what information was missing. A failed AI call produces a visible exception rather than a silent CRM decision.

A knowledge assistant that shows where every answer came from

Lets people ask questions in plain language across your approved documents, and answers with the specific document and section each statement came from.

The situation it addresses

Policies, procedures, product information, and operating guidance sit across many files and change over time. Nobody is sure which document holds the current answer. Searching by keyword fails when the question uses different words than the document. And a general AI tool will produce a confident answer with nothing behind it.

Where AI is required

Understanding a question asked in one set of words and finding the passage that answers it in another. That is meaning-matching, which keyword search cannot do.

How it runs
  1. 01You approve which documents may be used
  2. 02Documents read, divided into sections, source details kept
  3. 03Question asked in plain language
  4. 04Relevant passages found across approved documents only
  5. 05Weak or irrelevant matches discarded
  6. 06Answer written using only what was found
  7. 07Each statement linked to its document and section
  8. 08Not enough evidence? It says so, rather than inventing an answer

The governing principle: no material answer without evidence behind it.

What makes it trustworthy

The answer is built only from documents you have approved, never from what the AI happens to have learned elsewhere. Where the evidence is thin, it says so instead of producing something plausible. When a document is updated, only the affected content is refreshed. And the finding step is tested separately from the answering step, so a well-written answer cannot conceal the wrong source.

← Case studies Case study 03

Two-stage scoring across a large catalog

Takes thousands of supplier and product records in inconsistent formats, assesses the supplier before the product, and prepares approved items for publishing — with every rejection reason recorded.

The situation it addresses

Supplier catalogs arrive with inconsistent titles, categories, prices, shipping terms, and stock information. Reviewing products first creates repeated work, because one unsuitable supplier invalidates every product attached to it. Rejection reasons end up scattered across spreadsheets, so the same item gets reconsidered months later.

Where AI is required

Assessing commercial potential, usefulness, and how well a product is described — from text written by hundreds of different suppliers with no shared standard.

How it runs
  1. 01Catalog imported from source systems
  2. 02Records standardized — identifiers, categories, prices, stock
  3. 03Duplicate suppliers merged into one
  4. 04Suppliers scored on location, fulfillment, returns, and data quality
  5. 05Products from suppliers below threshold excluded before any product work
  6. 06Remaining products assessed on potential, margin, and description quality
  7. 07Pricing inputs calculated — cost, fees, shipping, tax, target margin
  8. 08A person approves
  9. 09Approved items created as drafts, never published automatically

Decision output: Approve · Review · Hold · Reject

What makes it trustworthy

Suppliers and products are scored separately, so a promising product cannot conceal an unreliable supplier. Every rejection carries a structured reason. Repeat runs process only what is new or changed. One faulty record does not halt the whole run. And nothing publishes without a person approving it.

Bringing scattered records into a single reporting layer

Collects records from several external systems on a schedule, reconciles them into one consistent history, and presents current state alongside how it came about.

The situation it addresses

Records arrive from different systems with different structures and update behavior. The current position is visible, but nothing explains how it came about or provides a dependable history. Repeated synchronization risks duplicates. Failed runs pass unnoticed.

How it runs
  1. 01Scheduled collection from each connected system
  2. 02Original records preserved exactly as received
  3. 03Mapped into one shared structure
  4. 04Entries created — running twice does not create duplicates
  5. 05Figures calculated and checked back against the source
  6. 06Differences flagged rather than absorbed
  7. 07Dashboard shows current state, history, and the status of every run
What makes it trustworthy

The original record is kept alongside the standardized one, so any figure can be traced back. Calculated results are checked against the source rather than assumed correct. Every run records what it processed, what it skipped, and what failed. A partial failure can be inspected and resumed without corrupting the history.

← Case studies Case study 05

Governed software delivery, where no step approves its own work

Splits delivery into separate roles — requirements, architecture, planning, building, review, testing, release readiness — each producing a defined document and each having to pass a check before the next stage begins.

The situation it addresses

When one AI interprets the request, writes the requirements, builds the work, reviews its own decisions, and declares it finished, contradictions can survive every stage untouched. The output looks complete. What is missing is any independent check that it does what was asked, and any record connecting the original requirement to the thing that was built.

For an organization paying for delivery rather than performing it, that is the whole difficulty: the work arrives, it looks finished, and there is no evidence to inspect.

Where AI is required

Turning stated intent into precise, testable requirements, and then — separately — reviewing the delivered work against them without inheriting the reasoning that produced it.

How it runs
  1. 01Intent, constraints, and existing systems collected
  2. 02Requirements written — scope, rules, outcomes, acceptance criteria
  3. 03Requirements challenged — ambiguity, conflicts, and gaps identified
  4. 04Architecture and boundaries defined
  5. 05Work broken into ordered, testable packages
  6. 06Built — one bounded change at a time
  7. 07Reviewed independently — by a role that did not build it
  8. 08Tested against the requirements and the failure cases
  9. 09Every requirement traced to what was built and how it was verified
  10. 10Release readiness — a person accepts, rejects, or returns it

The governing principle: no step approves its own critical output.

What makes it trustworthy

The roles are genuinely separate — requirements, building, review, and testing are not collapsed into one instruction. Every requirement carries an identifier that connects it to what was built and how it was verified, so "is this actually finished" has an evidence-based answer. A missing or invalid document stops the work rather than passing quietly downstream. Progress is saved, so a failure resumes from the last good stage instead of restarting. And scope, architecture exceptions, and release decisions stay with a person.

What you get from it as the buyer

A record you can inspect. Not a report that the work is done, but the requirement, the review that challenged it, the test that verified it, and the decision that accepted it.

Example

Inquiry to scoped proposal

A commercial fit-out contractor receives inquiries as long, unstructured emails, often with drawings attached. Each one is read by an estimator, summarized, entered into the CRM, and turned into a proposal — a sequence of two to three hours before anyone has priced anything.

The AI-requiring steps

Reading a discursive email and identifying scope, timescale, site constraints, and what has not been specified; then producing a first-pass proposal in the firm's own structure and language.

How it runs

The inquiry is read and reduced to a structured record — scope, location, indicative value, timescale, and an explicit list of what is still unknown. That record populates the CRM. Scope: three AI steps, four connected systems, tested against 40 historical inquiries. Four to six weeks.

Example

An accountancy practice receives client bookkeeping in whatever form it arrives — photographs, PDFs, forwarded emails, occasionally a shoebox. Junior staff extract the details and code each item.

Reading documents with no consistent layout, and deciding the correct account code from a supplier name and description that vary between clients.

How it runs

Documents arriving in a monitored inbox are read and the supplier, date, net, tax, and total extracted. Approved entries post to Xero or QuickBooks.

Scope: two AI steps, three connected systems, tested against 50 historical documents including deliberately poor scans. Four to six weeks.

Where the sequence is genuinely one task, Lite covers it for less. Where it spans five or more systems or several teams, that is Advanced.

Size 03

Advanced — complex and business-critical AI workflows

The work

A sequence where several steps involve judgment, drafting, checking, or analysis, spanning three to six systems, with multiple roles involved and output that reaches clients or drives decisions.

Why this needs AI

The sequence carries genuine variation, and the cases outside the normal pattern have to be identified and routed rather than forced through.

Typical technology

Claude or OpenAI for classification, extraction, and drafting · n8n or Make for the sequence and routing · your project, finance, document, and CRM systems · a reporting layer where needed.

What the workflow looks like
  1. 01Documents and data arrive from several systems
  2. 02AI classifies, extracts, and cross-checks against your standards
  3. 03Anything outside the pattern routed to a named person, with the reason
  4. 04AI drafts the output: report, response, assessment, or summary
  5. 05Role-based review — the right person checks the right part
  6. 06Published to the client-facing system, with a record of what was decided and why
Example

Candidate screening and shortlist notes

A recruitment agency receives large volumes of applications per role. Consultants read each CV against the brief, form a view, and write shortlist notes for the client.

The AI-requiring steps

Reading a CV written in any format and assessing it against a specific brief; recognizing that unrelated job titles describe relevant experience; and writing a shortlist note in the consultant's own voice.

How it runs

Applications are read against the role brief and scored on the criteria the agency actually uses, with the evidence for each score quoted from the CV rather than asserted. Anything borderline is flagged for a consultant rather than rejected. Shortlist notes are drafted in the agency's house style. Every rejection remains a human decision — the workflow ranks and evidences, it does not decline anyone.

Scope: four AI steps, five connected systems, deliberate testing against historical applications including ones the agency judged marginal. Six to ten weeks with a phased rollout by desk.

Example

Policy renewal review

An insurance brokerage reviews client policies ahead of renewal. Each review means reading the existing policy, the client's updated details, and the market alternatives, then producing a recommendation.

The AI-requiring steps

Comparing policy wordings that express the same cover differently; identifying where a client's circumstances have changed in a way that affects cover; and drafting a recommendation with reasoning a client can follow.

How it runs

The current policy, claims history, and updated client information are read and reduced to a structured comparison. Changes in exposure are identified and flagged. A renewal recommendation is drafted with the reasoning set out. Any recommendation involving a coverage change is reviewed by a broker before it reaches the client, and anything the workflow cannot assess confidently is routed with the reason rather than assumed.

Scope: four AI steps, six connected systems, deep testing against historical renewals including disputed ones. Six to ten weeks.

Example

Delivery and margin monitoring

A marketing agency runs client work across a project system, a time tracker, and accounting software. Whether a job is running below margin becomes clear when it is invoiced, which is too late to act.

The AI-requiring steps

Reading project updates, notes, and client correspondence to identify scope movement that has not been formally recorded — the phrase in an email that means the job has grown but no change order was raised.

How it runs

Activity across the three systems is assembled weekly. Time booked is compared against fee. Written updates and correspondence are read for signals of scope movement. The output is a weekly view of which jobs are drifting, which are running below expected margin, and which have grown without a change order — with the evidence quoted rather than asserted.

Scope: three AI steps, five connected systems, tested against twelve months of historical project data. Six to ten weeks.

Where a sequence spans two to four systems and one team, Standard covers it in four to six weeks.

Model providers release updates.

Output that was consistent shifts in tone or structure.

Your own standards change.

What counted as a good draft last year is not what you want now.

None of these stop it working. They move the output gradually — from reliable, to occasionally needing an edit, to needing a careful check every time. At that point people quietly return to doing the work by hand, and the conclusion becomes that AI did not suit the business.

AI vs automation

AI automation vs traditional automation — where each one belongs

Most of what a business wants to automate does not need AI at all. Knowing which steps do is the difference between a workflow that holds up and one that quietly stops being trusted.

Use a rule

Traditional automation

The input arrives in the same shape every time, and the decision can be written down in advance. Faster, cheaper, and more reliable than AI — and it does exactly the same thing on Tuesday as it did on Monday.

    Moving a field from one system to another. Sending a reminder when a date passes. Applying a discount when a value exceeds a threshold. Creating a record when a form is submitted.
Use AI

AI automation

The input arrives as free text, speech, or documents that vary every time, and a person currently has to read it before anything can happen. No rule can be written for what it means.

    Reading a discursive email and identifying what is being asked for. Extracting the same fields from documents with no consistent layout. Deciding what a request is actually about rather than which keyword it contains. Drafting in your house style from material already in your systems.

Almost every workflow we build uses both: rules, then an AI step, then rules again, with a review point before anything is used.

How to tell

Four questions to ask about any step

01

Does the input arrive in the same shape every time?

If yes, a rule will handle it. If it arrives as a paragraph someone wrote, it will not.

02

Could you write the decision down as instructions?

If a competent new starter could follow a one-page rule sheet and get it right every time, it is automation, not AI.

03

What happens when the step gets it wrong?

The higher the cost of a wrong answer, the more the design is about routing and review rather than about the AI itself.

04

How often does it happen?

A step that needs judgment but runs twice a month is rarely worth building. Volume is what makes the effort repay.

Plainly

Where we will not put AI

    Anywhere a simple rule would do the job more cheaply and more reliably. On a calculation. Arithmetic belongs in the system that already does it. On a decision that needs judgment a person should be making. On a step that runs rarely enough that the build will never repay itself.
